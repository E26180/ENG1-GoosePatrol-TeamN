Risk1 – Part a: risk management process

a1. Process

We follow the risk management process described by Sommerville [1], in four steps that repeat for the whole project:

  • Identification. At the kick-off meeting in week 1 the team created a Risk and Quality Manager role to set up and look after the register, which started from a template in the repository (docs/templates). Risks were then collected from the product brief, the client meeting of 14 October, and our own lack of experience with libGDX and with an eight-person team. We used Sommerville’s categories as a checklist so that we did not only think of technical problems: project risks (schedule and resources), product risks (the quality of the game), and business risks (the client and the university). Because this is a student team we split out technical, people and legal risks as well, since they need different owners.
  • Analysis. Each risk gets a likelihood and a severity on a three-point scale (L, M, H), and a level from the matrix below. The level decides how much attention a risk gets: High risks should be checked at every team meeting and have a mitigation already in use, Medium risks need a planned mitigation, and Low risks are watched.
  • Planning. Every risk has a mitigation that either avoids it, reduces its likelihood or its effect, or sets out a contingency plan for when it happens. Each risk also has one named owner, who checks it and acts on early signs.
  • Monitoring. The register is a living document. Owners report changes on the team Discord and at team meetings; a risk whose likelihood or severity changes is re-rated, and new risks are added with the next free ID. The last column records the state of each risk at the latest review (9 November 2026), including the risks that actually happened and how the mitigation worked.

a2. Format and level of detail, and why

MLowMediumHigh
HMediumHighHigh
  • A table with one row per risk. It can be scanned at a meeting, sorted by owner or level, and kept under version control with the rest of the project, so earlier versions can be compared.
  • Columns. ID (never reused, so meeting notes and commits can refer to a risk), type, a description that starts with a short name and says what could happen and what it would cause, likelihood, severity, level, owner, mitigation, and status at the last review.
  • Three-point scales. None of us has run a project like this before, so our estimates are rough; a five-point or percentage scale would suggest a precision we do not have. Three levels are quick to agree on in a meeting and are enough to decide what to act on first.
  • One owner per risk. With eight people, a risk owned by “the team” is owned by no one. Owners are matched to roles, for example the developer of the shared interfaces owns integration (R4).
  • Letters as well as colour. Levels are written as words and ratings as letters, with colour only as an extra cue, so the register can be read by colour-blind team members and when printed in black and white.
  • Project-specific risks. As well as risks common to any software project, the register includes risks that come from this client: the no-violence rule (R15), accessibility (R16), a game that is too hard for first-time players (R17) and running on all three operating systems (R18).
